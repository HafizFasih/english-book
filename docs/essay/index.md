# Chapter 11 — Essay Writing

An **essay** is a structured piece of writing that argues or explores a topic through a logical, connected series of paragraphs. Unlike a paragraph, which covers one idea, an essay develops multiple related arguments around a central thesis.

---

## 11.1 General Essay Outline

```
I. Introduction
   a) Hook / attention grabber
   b) Background information / definitions
   c) Thesis statement

II. Body — Paragraph 1
   a) Topic sentence (argument supporting your thesis)
   b) Supporting evidence — data, facts, examples
   c) Explanation of how this relates to your thesis

III. Body — Paragraph 2
   a) Topic sentence (another argument)
   b) Supporting evidence
   c) Explanation

IV. Body — Paragraph 3
   a) Topic sentence (further argument or counterargument)
   b) Supporting evidence or refutation of counterargument
   c) Explanation

V. Conclusion
   a) Summary of all main points
   b) Restatement of thesis (in new words)
   c) Call to action / final thought for the reader
```

## 11.2 Types of Essay

### Descriptive Essay

A descriptive essay asks you to describe something — an object, person, place, experience, emotion, or situation. The goal is to create a vivid impression in the reader's mind.

**Guidelines for writing a descriptive essay:**

- **Brainstorm first** — jot down sensory words before you begin
- **Use clear and concise language** — choose words for their relevance and precision
- **Choose vivid language** — prefer *stallion* over *horse*, *tempestuous* over *violent*, *miserly* over *cheap*
- **Engage the senses** — describe how things look, smell, feel, sound, and taste
- **Connect emotionally** — if you can describe feelings related to your topic, you connect with the reader on a deeper level
- **Leave a clear impression** — the reader should be able to form a mental image or feeling
- **Be organised** — avoid an incoherent rambling of emotions; present a logical description

> *If the reader is unable to clearly form an impression of the thing you are describing, try again.*

---

### Narrative Essay

A narrative essay tells a story. These essays are often anecdotal, experiential, and personal — they allow students to express themselves creatively.

**Guidelines for writing a narrative essay:**

- **Include all parts of a story** — introduction, plot, characters, setting, climax, and conclusion
- **Use vivid details and sensory language** — make the reader feel present
- **Write in first person** (usually) — *I walked into the room…*
- **Maintain a clear chronological or thematic order**
- **Have a clear purpose** — the story should illustrate a point or convey a lesson

---

### Narrative Essay (continued)

**Additional guidelines:**

- **When would a narrative essay not be written as a story?** A book report, for instance, would focus on providing an informative narrative rather than following the pattern of a story.
- **The essay should have a purpose.** Make a point — think of this as the thesis of your story. If there is no point to what you are narrating, why narrate it at all?
- **The essay should be written from a clear point of view.** It is common for narrative essays to be written from the standpoint of the author, but this is not the only perspective to consider.
- **Use clear and concise language.** Much like the descriptive essay, narrative essays are effective when the language is carefully and artfully chosen.
- **The use of the first person pronoun 'I' is welcomed** — but do not overuse it for lack of clearer diction.
- **Be organised.** Have a clear introduction that sets the tone for the remainder of the essay. Do not leave the reader guessing about the purpose of your narrative.

---

### Argumentative / Persuasive Essay

An argumentative essay takes a position on a debatable topic and defends it using evidence and logic.

**Key features:**

- A clear, specific, and debatable **thesis statement**
- **Multiple arguments** supported by evidence (facts, statistics, examples, expert opinion)
- **Acknowledgement and refutation of counterarguments**
- A persuasive, confident tone
- A strong conclusion with a call to action

**Structure of the Argumentative Essay:**

**1. A clear, concise, and defined thesis statement in the first paragraph.** Set the context by reviewing the topic generally, explain why the topic is important (**exigence**), then present the thesis. The thesis must be appropriately narrowed.

**2. Clear and logical transitions between the introduction, body, and conclusion.** Transitions are the mortar that holds the essay together — they wrap up the idea from the previous section and introduce the next.

**3. Body paragraphs with evidential support.** Each paragraph covers one general idea. Each paragraph must connect logically to the thesis. It is also important to explain **how and why** the evidence supports the thesis (**warrant**). One or two paragraphs should address conflicting opinions — note how they may not be well informed or may be out of date, rather than dismissing them outright.

**4. Evidential support (factual, logical, statistical, or anecdotal).** Use well-researched, accurate, and current information. It is unethical to exclude evidence that does not support the thesis.

**5. A conclusion that readdresses the thesis in light of the evidence provided.** Synthesise the body's information. Do not introduce any new information. Restate why the topic is important, review the main points, and review your thesis.

**The Five-Paragraph Argumentative Essay:**
1. An introductory paragraph
2. Three evidentiary body paragraphs (which may include discussion of opposing views)
3. A conclusion

### Persuasive Essay Structure

**Introduction:**
- Hook — an interesting first sentence
- Background information — context; familiarise the reader with the topic
- Definitions — define unfamiliar terms
- Thesis — a clear, concise statement of your main argument; a roadmap for the essay

**Each Body Paragraph:**
- Only one point to support your thesis per paragraph
- Topic sentence (reflects the main idea; links back to the thesis)
- Evidence — information from a reliable outside source
- Analysis — show how the evidence supports your argument

**Conclusion:**
- Briefly sum up the main point
- Establish significance
- Give the reader food for thought

### Expository Essay

An expository essay requires the student to investigate an idea, evaluate evidence, expound on it, and set forth an argument in a clear and concise manner. This can be accomplished through comparison and contrast, definition, example, analysis of cause and effect, etc.

*Note: This genre is commonly assigned as a tool for classroom evaluation and is often found in various exam formats.*

**Key features:**

- Neutral, objective tone
- Factual information from reliable sources
- Clear logical structure
- No personal opinions or bias

**Structure of the Expository Essay:**

1. **A clear, concise, and defined thesis statement** in the first paragraph — narrowed to the assignment guidelines.
2. **Clear and logical transitions** between the introduction, body, and conclusion.
3. **Body paragraphs with evidential support** — each paragraph limited to the exposition of one general idea; each connected logically to the thesis.
4. **Evidential support** — often expository essays are written with little preparation, so they do not typically allow for a great deal of statistical or factual evidence.
5. **A bit of creativity** — though creativity is not always associated with essay writing, it is an art form. Try not to get stuck on the formulaic nature of expository writing at the expense of writing something interesting.
6. **A conclusion that readdresses the thesis** in light of the evidence — synthesise, do not merely restate.

**The Five-Paragraph Expository Essay:**
1. An introductory paragraph
2. Three evidentiary body paragraphs
3. A conclusion

## 11.3 The Introduction

A strong introduction has three components:

1. **Hook** — grabs the reader's attention (see Chapter 3 for hook types)
2. **Background** — provides context and necessary definitions
3. **Thesis** — states your central argument clearly

### Sample Introduction

> *From the time we are young, teachers often stand out as some of the most influential figures in our lives, shaping not only what we know but also how we think and grow. The profession itself carries a unique blend of intellectual challenge and human connection, requiring both knowledge and empathy. For me, the decision to become a teacher was not a sudden choice but rather the result of several deeply rooted passions that guided me toward this path. My love for the English language, my innate interest in how people learn, and my desire to help other people all came together to inspire my journey into teaching.*

---

## 11.4 The Conclusion

The conclusion is not a place to introduce new arguments. It should:

- **Summarise** the main points made in the body
- **Restate the thesis** in fresh language
- **End memorably** — with a call to action, a forward-looking statement, or a powerful final sentence

---

## 11.5 Essay Writing Tips

| Tip | Why It Matters |
|-----|----------------|
| Plan before you write | Pre-writing prevents disorganised essays |
| One idea per paragraph | Keeps the essay focused and readable |
| Use transitions between paragraphs | Ensures the essay flows as a coherent whole |
| Vary sentence structure | Avoids monotony; keeps the reader engaged |
| Avoid clichés and vague generalisations | Weakens your argument |
| Cite your sources | Adds credibility and avoids plagiarism |
| Proofread | Errors undermine your entire argument |
