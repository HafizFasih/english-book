# Chapter 3 — Pre-Writing Strategies

Pre-writing is an informal process that allows you to explore ideas before you begin drafting. You don't need to worry about organisation or mechanics at this stage — the goal is simply to generate ideas.

There are five main pre-writing strategies:

1. Free Writing / Focused Free Writing
2. Brainstorming / Mind-Mapping
3. Clustering
4. Questioning
5. Outlining

---

## 3.1 Free Writing

**Free writing** means writing continuously for a set time (e.g., 15 minutes) or to a set length (e.g., one page) without stopping — regardless of grammar, spelling, or organisation. Just keep your pen (or fingers) moving. This is most useful for creative writing or when you are completely stuck.

**Focused free writing** is the same technique but applied to a specific topic. Even if you think you know nothing about the subject, write whatever comes to mind: what you think it means, what you imagine, what questions it raises.

### How to use it

> *Think or write "I am…" then write as much as you can.*
> - I am a student (or fill in your occupation).
> - I am a daughter / son / brother / sister.
> - I am an animal lover.
>
> Write down all of the things associated with that part of yourself. Allow your ideas to flow, even if they become unrelated to the original idea.

---

## 3.2 Brainstorming / Mind-Mapping

**Brainstorming** is creating a list of ideas in response to a problem or question. The ideas don't need to be related to each other — they only need to be related to the topic.

There are two main approaches:

1. **Posing and answering questions:** Who? What? Where? When? Why? How?
2. **Gathering a list of ideas** — for example, if your topic is your favourite food, list all the sensory information: what does it look, feel, taste, smell, and sound like?

### Example — Topic: "Apples"

- My favourite type: Honey Crisp
- Colour: pinkish-red skin with orangey yellow
- Feel: smooth-skinned
- Sound when bitten: a snap, then a crunch

## 3.3 Questioning Strategy

This strategy helps you capture the most important information about a topic by answering six basic questions.

### The Six Questions

| Question | What to Write |
|----------|--------------|
| **Who?** | The people involved or affected |
| **What?** | The main issue or event |
| **Where?** | The setting or context |
| **When?** | The time frame |
| **Why?** | The reason or significance |
| **How?** | The method or process |

### Example in Action

*Topic: What is the impact of traditional ecological knowledge on environmental management?*

- **Who?** The Dene and Kessi tribes from two different ecosystems were impacted by European colonisers and their fire management policies.
- **What?** Consider the impact of fire on peoples in both environments.
- **Where?** Canadian policies and historical data compared to African policies and historical data.
- **When?** As far back as the last ice age; focused on the impact of colonisation and current implications.
- **Why?** The knowledge from indigenous peoples provides important insights into how to improve current fire practices.
- **How?** By examining historical and current records.

## 3.4 Clustering

**Clustering** (also called mind-mapping) uses a visual map to shape ideas and explore how they relate to each other.

**How to cluster:**
1. Write your topic in a bubble in the centre of the page.
2. Draw lines outward to related ideas.
3. From each related idea, draw further lines to supporting details.
4. The result is a spider-web diagram showing how all your ideas connect.

## 3.5 Outlining

An **outline** is a general plan of the material to be presented in a paper or speech. It shows:
- The **order** of topics
- The **relative importance** of each
- The **relationship** between different parts

### Basic Essay Outline Structure

```
I.   Introduction
     A. Hook / attention grabber
     B. Background information
     C. Thesis statement

II.  Body Paragraph 1
     A. Topic sentence
     B. Supporting evidence / details
     C. Explanation / analysis

III. Body Paragraph 2
     A. Topic sentence
     B. Supporting evidence / details
     C. Explanation / analysis

IV.  Body Paragraph 3
     A. Topic sentence / counterargument
     B. Supporting evidence
     C. Explanation / refutation

V.   Conclusion
     A. Summary of main points
     B. Restatement of thesis
     C. Call to action / final thought
```

## 3.6 Hook Statements

The **hook** is the very first sentence of your essay — its purpose is to capture the reader's attention immediately.

:::tip
Never begin an essay with *"In this essay, I will…"* or *"This essay is about…"* These are weak openings that tell the reader nothing interesting.
:::

### Types of Hooks

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Interesting Question** | Ask a question the reader must read on to answer | *Have you ever felt what it's like to have your identity taken away from you?* |
| **Strong Statement** | A bold claim directly related to your topic | *Social media has done more harm to teenagers than any previous technology in history.* |
| **Fact / Statistic** | A surprising or alarming fact from a credible source | *Pakistan loses over $3.8 billion annually to climate-related disasters.* |
| **Metaphor / Simile** | A creative comparison to your topic | *Language is a map — without it, we are permanently lost.* |
| **Story / Anecdote** | A brief personal or observed story | *The morning I received my rejection letter, I did not cry — I made a plan.* |
| **Description** | A vivid scene to draw the reader in | *The air tasted of smoke and ash as we drove through the flood-ravaged district.* |
| **Quotation** | A powerful, relevant quotation with attribution | *"Education is the most powerful weapon which you can use to change the world." — Nelson Mandela* |

### Hook Examples: Effective vs. Ineffective

| Effective | Ineffective |
|-----------|-------------|
| *Many times I felt eyes on me as I walked alone down the street.* | *My essay topic is about how women don't feel safe walking alone.* |
| *When asked to name a pet, most people think of cats and dogs — but have they ever considered chickens?* | *This essay is going to be about backyard chickens.* |

## 3.7 Thesis Statements

A **thesis statement** is the central argument of your essay — a single, clear, debatable claim that tells the reader what you are going to argue and why.

### Characteristics of a Strong Thesis
- **Specific** — not vague or too broad
- **Debatable** — not an obvious fact
- **Focused** — covers the exact scope of your essay
- **Supportable** — you can back it with evidence

### Examples

| Weak Thesis | Strong Thesis |
|-------------|---------------|
| *Dogs are good pets.* | *Dogs contribute to their owner's well-being and health by encouraging regular exercise and social interaction.* |
| *School uniforms are controversial.* | *School uniforms should be banned in all schools because they restrict individuality and free expression.* |

### Sample Introduction Using a Thesis

> *From the time we are young, teachers often stand out as some of the most influential figures in our lives, shaping not only what we know but also how we think and grow. The profession itself carries a unique blend of intellectual challenge and human connection, requiring both knowledge and empathy. For me, the decision to become a teacher was not a sudden choice but rather the result of several deeply rooted passions that guided me toward this path. My love for the English language, my innate interest in how people learn, and my desire to help other people all came together to inspire my journey into teaching.*

**Structure breakdown:**
- **Hook:** Universal statement about teachers' influence
- **Background:** Nature of the profession
- **Thesis:** *"My love for the English language, my innate interest in how people learn, and my desire to help other people..."*
