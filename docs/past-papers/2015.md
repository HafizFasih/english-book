## Q2 — Reading Comprehension *(12 marks)*

### Passage: *Radio, Cinema and Mass Culture*

Radio and cinema had one novelty in common: they were forms of communication which dispensed with the written except for the caption in silent films. The written word had gone hand in hand with civilization from the beginning. Now, theoretically, illiterates could be as well-informed about the world as the best-read man. Reading might have been expected to decline as a result, but this did not happen. Perhaps the habit was too inbred. Besides, primary education, now almost universal in Europe, made literacy also universal. Far from declining, the written, or rather the printed word triumphed as never before.

Newspapers, which had greatly increased their circulation during the First World War, continued to do so after it. Great Britain, which carried the process furthest, the press by 1930 ranked twelfth among British industries, ahead of shipbuilding. Newspapers counted their readers by millions where they had previously counted by thousands. They had bigger headlines, shorter paragraphs, simpler writing. They derived their incomes mainly from advertisements, not from the halfpennies or pennies paid by readers. The decisive figure was the proprietor—Northcliffe and Beaverbrook in Great Britain, Hugenberg in Germany—not the editor. Nearly all the great newspapers were conservative in character, and often conservative in allegiance. They were among the most materialistic elements in a materialistic age. Nevertheless, they provided more news than had been provided by even the most esteemed newspapers of a staider past.

The newspapers, like the cinema and usually radio, expressed popular culture, and observed talk as though this were the only culture which now existed. The flood of the mass-age was supposed to have submerged the standard of previous times, but this was far from being the case. There was middle culture and high culture — distinctions between them resting on levels of sophistication (middlebrow and highbrow) not on class. The middlebrow culture was the least interesting, repetition of past patterns interspersed with lamentations against anything new. Those who condemned James Joyce or Picasso also disapproved of the cinema. The middlebrows felt more menaced than before, hence the intolerance which contrasted oddly with their professions of liberalism. Original artists and thinkers were constantly, though ineffectually, harassed.

Nevertheless, this was an age of intellectual and artistic activity. Paris reached perhaps its highest point as the cultural capital of Europe. English artists had always congregated there, though fewer Germans did so as a result of the First World War. There were now also more Russians, usually refugees, and more Americans — no longer there merely to learn to admire, but there to lead and to create. Ernest Hemingway and Gertrude Stein counted as decisive figures in European literature. Her writings expressed the spirit of subversion which now shaped much of European art.

In the nineteenth century, science had powerfully supported rationalistic philosophy. Scientists were expected to discover general laws of increasing certainty. In the twentieth century, scientists began to doubt the finality of their own conclusions, just when ordinary men came to believe that reason had triumphed. Psychology reinforced this outlook. Freud discovered the unconscious and taught that man's apparently rational acts were in fact often determined by what happened to him before the age of five. Jung postulated a collective unconscious and a folk-memory going back for centuries. These systems, when vulgarized, struck an immense blow against reason.

### Q2.B — Short Answers *(5 marks)*

- **a)** What is meant by "novelty in common" which radio and cinema share?
- **b)** Mention a few indicators of the rise of newspaper industry after the First World War.
- **c)** What was the difference between middle and high culture?
- **d)** What, according to the author, was the fate of reason in the era of intellectual and artistic activity?
- **e)** In the fifth line of the last paragraph, what does "this outlook" refer to?

<details>
<summary>View Answers</summary>

**a)** The "novelty in common" that radio and cinema shared was that both communicated without the need for the written word. They could reach people who could not read, which was something newspapers and books could not do. This was a completely new development in the history of communication.

**b)** Several indicators show the rise of the newspaper industry after World War I: newspapers greatly increased their circulation; in Britain, the press ranked twelfth among all industries by 1930; readership rose from thousands to millions; newspapers adopted bigger headlines, shorter paragraphs, and simpler writing to attract a wider audience.

**c)** Both middle culture (middlebrow) and high culture (highbrow) existed alongside popular mass culture. The difference between them was not based on class or wealth, but on the level of intellectual sophistication. High culture was associated with more complex and serious art and ideas, while middle culture was more repetitive and resistant to new ideas.

**d)** According to the author, reason was undermined during this era. Scientists themselves began to doubt their own conclusions. Freud showed that human behavior was driven by unconscious forces, not pure reason. Jung proposed a collective unconscious. These ideas, when spread to the general public, struck a powerful blow against the belief in human reason that had dominated Western thought since the Renaissance.

**e)** "This outlook" in the last paragraph refers to the psychological outlook or perspective that was introduced by Freud — the idea that human beings are not driven by reason but by unconscious desires and impulses formed in early childhood. Jung's theories reinforced this same view.

</details>

## Q3 — Précis Writing *(12 marks)*

Draft a précis of the passage in about **250 words** and give it a suitable title.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

**Title: The Rise of Mass Media and the Challenge to Reason**

Radio and cinema had introduced a new form of mass communication that did not require literacy, theoretically making knowledge equally accessible to everyone. However, reading did not decline as expected; instead, the printed word grew stronger. Newspapers greatly expanded their readership after the First World War and became powerful industries, earning their income mainly from advertisements. Their content became simpler and more popular, controlled by proprietors rather than editors.

While these mass media spread popular culture, they coexisted with middle and high culture, distinguished not by class but by intellectual sophistication. Middlebrow culture, however, was resistant to new ideas and showed intolerance toward original thinkers and artists. Despite this, it was an age of considerable intellectual and artistic achievement, with Paris as its cultural center, attracting American writers and artists who contributed significantly to European literature and art.

In the realm of ideas, the era witnessed a major challenge to rationalism. Scientists began questioning their own certainties, while psychologists like Freud and Jung proposed that human behavior was shaped by unconscious forces rather than reason. Freud argued that childhood experiences determined adult actions, while Jung proposed the concept of a collective unconscious. When simplified and popularized, these theories seriously weakened the public's belief in reason as the guiding force of human life.

*(Word count: 208)*

</details>

## Q5 — Paragraph Writing *(10 marks)*

Write a paragraph on **"Advances in Technology"**.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

<u>Advances in technology have transformed every aspect of human life, making the world faster, more connected, and more efficient than ever before.</u> Just a few decades ago, people relied on letters and landline telephones to communicate. **Today**, a person in Karachi can video call someone in London in seconds using a smartphone. In the field of medicine, technology has made it possible to detect and treat diseases that were once considered fatal. **Furthermore**, in education, students now have access to thousands of books and courses online, making quality learning available to anyone with an internet connection. **However**, these advances come with challenges as well — concerns about privacy, addiction to screens, and the loss of jobs due to automation are all serious issues that need to be addressed. **In conclusion**, while technology has brought remarkable benefits to humanity, it is our responsibility to use it wisely and ensure that its advantages are shared equally across society.

## Q6 — Essay Writing *(12 marks)*

Write an essay of about **350 words** on ANY ONE topic. Underline the **thesis statement** and at least **three transition signals**.

- a) Role of science and technology in education.
- b) Nuclear weapons maintain world peace. Do you agree or disagree?
- c) Reasons and impact of Child labour on society.

<details>
<summary>View Answer — Topic (c): Reasons and Impact of Child Labour on Society</summary>

**Brainstorming:** Poverty / Lack of education / Cheap labour / Physical harm / Loss of childhood / Long-term damage

---

**Reasons and Impact of Child Labour on Society**

Every day, millions of children wake up not to go to school, but to factories, fields, and roadside workshops where they will spend hours doing dangerous, exhausting work for very little money. This is the reality of child labour — one of the most serious social problems in the world. <u>Child labour is primarily caused by poverty and lack of education, and its impact on children and society is deeply damaging and long-lasting.</u>

<u>The main reason behind child labour is extreme poverty.</u> When families cannot afford basic necessities, parents have no choice but to send their children to work. In many developing countries, a child's wages contribute significantly to household income. <u>Furthermore</u>, the lack of free and compulsory education means that children who are not in school have nothing productive to do, making them vulnerable to exploitation by employers who pay very low wages.

<u>The impact of child labour on individual children is devastating.</u> Children who work instead of studying miss out on education, which permanently limits their career opportunities. Physical labour at a young age causes serious health problems — injuries, deformities, and chronic illnesses. <u>In addition</u>, children in labour are deprived of play, rest, and the emotional development that comes with a normal childhood. They grow up without the skills or confidence needed to succeed in adult life.

<u>At the societal level, child labour perpetuates a cycle of poverty.</u> Uneducated, unskilled adults earn low wages and live in poverty, making them likely to send their own children to work as well. This cycle is very difficult to break without strong government intervention.

In conclusion, child labour is both a cause and a consequence of poverty. <u>To eliminate it, governments must enforce compulsory education laws, provide financial support to poor families, and strictly punish employers who hire children.</u> Only then can every child enjoy the childhood they deserve.

*(Word count: 330)*

</details>

## Q7 — Contextual Vocabulary *(12 marks)*

Use the context to determine the meaning of each highlighted word. Explain the meaning in your own words and use the word in your own sentence.

- **a)** In the early 1600s, a dangerous trip across the Atlantic Ocean was a **daunting** idea.
- **b)** The original Pilgrims called themselves the "Saints" and referred to others as the **voyage** companions.
- **c)** Most of America's Founding Fathers did not believe in women's **suffrage**.
- **d)** No matter where you go, the Internet is truly **ubiquitous**.
- **e)** Speaking rudely to the judges was **rash** behavior.
- **f)** Some people are always **bashing** the president.

<details>
<summary>View Answers</summary>

**a) Daunting** — something that seems very difficult, frightening, or discouraging
*Sentence:* The final exam seemed daunting, but after weeks of preparation, I felt confident.

**b) Voyage** — a long journey, especially by sea or through space
*Sentence:* The first voyage to the moon was one of the greatest achievements in human history.

**c) Suffrage** — the right to vote in elections
*Sentence:* Women fought for many decades to gain suffrage in countries around the world.

**d) Ubiquitous** — present or found everywhere at the same time
*Sentence:* Smartphones have become so ubiquitous that it is rare to see a person without one.

**e) Rash** — acting quickly and carelessly without thinking about the consequences
*Sentence:* It was rash of him to quit his job without having another one lined up.

**f) Bashing** — criticizing someone strongly and repeatedly in an unfair way
*Sentence:* Instead of bashing each other online, politicians should focus on solving real problems.

</details>
