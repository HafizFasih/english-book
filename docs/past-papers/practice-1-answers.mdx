### Q1.2 — Why do people procrastinate? *(3 marks)*

According to the passage, procrastination is not a time management problem — it is an **emotion regulation problem**. When a task causes feelings like anxiety, boredom, or self-doubt, people naturally avoid it to feel better in the short term. The brain's limbic system, which seeks immediate pleasure, overpowers the prefrontal cortex, which is responsible for long-term planning. As a result, a person chooses something comfortable and easy, like scrolling social media, over an important but difficult task, like writing an assignment.

---

### Q1.3 — Passive vs Active Procrastinator *(3 marks)*

A **passive procrastinator** delays tasks because they cannot decide what to do or feel unable to act. They experience stress and regret as a result.

An **active procrastinator** deliberately chooses to delay because they believe they work better under pressure. They are more in control and often produce quality results at the last minute while experiencing less stress.

However, active procrastination is still risky because unexpected events — illness, power cuts, emergencies — can ruin even the best last-minute plans.

---

### Q1.4 — Strategies to Overcome Procrastination *(3 marks)*

The author suggests the following strategies:
1. **Break large tasks** into smaller, manageable steps
2. **Use the two-minute rule** — start with just two focused minutes to build momentum
3. **Set specific deadlines** to create accountability
4. **Remove digital distractions** such as social media apps while working
5. **Reward yourself** after completing each small step to reinforce the habit

## Question 2 — Précis Writing *(10 marks)*

**Title: Understanding and Overcoming Procrastination**

Procrastination — the habit of delaying important tasks — was found by researchers to be not a time management failure but an emotional regulation problem. Studies had shown that students who procrastinated performed worse academically and experienced greater stress. The brain's pleasure-seeking system overcame its planning function, causing individuals to choose short-term comfort over long-term achievement. Researchers distinguished between passive procrastinators, who delayed tasks due to indecision, and active procrastinators, who performed well under pressure but remained at risk if unexpected events arose. To overcome procrastination, experts recommended breaking tasks into small steps, applying the two-minute rule, eliminating distractions, setting clear deadlines, and rewarding oneself after each step.

**Online Education Can Never Fully Replace Traditional Classroom Learning**

During the COVID-19 pandemic, millions of students around the world were forced to attend school and university through screens. While online learning kept education running during a crisis, it also revealed something important: a screen can transmit information, but it cannot replicate the full experience of being in a classroom. <u>Online education, despite its convenience and accessibility, can never fully replace traditional classroom learning because it lacks human connection, practical experience, and the structured environment essential for deep learning.</u>

<u>One of the most significant limitations of online education is the absence of real human interaction.</u> In a physical classroom, a student can ask a question and receive an instant, personalised response. They can observe a teacher's body language, engage in spontaneous discussions, and learn from classmates' different perspectives. <u>Furthermore</u>, the social bonds formed in a classroom — friendships, study groups, mentorship — are difficult to replicate through a screen. Research consistently shows that students learn better when they feel connected to their teachers and peers.

<u>Another critical drawback is the lack of practical, hands-on training.</u> Engineering, medicine, chemistry, and architecture all require laboratory work, fieldwork, and hands-on projects that simply cannot be done online. A medical student cannot learn surgery by watching a video. An engineering student cannot understand circuit behaviour without physically building one. These practical skills are the foundation of professional competence, and no online platform can replace them.

<u>Supporters of online education argue that it is more flexible, affordable, and accessible than the traditional model</u>, allowing students in remote areas to attend world-class courses without travelling. This is a genuine and important benefit. <u>However</u>, accessibility alone does not equal quality. An online degree may open a door, but the depth of learning, critical thinking, and professional preparation that comes from a structured campus environment cannot be replicated by a video lecture. The two models serve different needs — one should not replace the other.

In conclusion, online education is a powerful complement to traditional learning, but it will never replace it entirely. <u>A classroom is more than a location — it is a community of learning, and no screen can fully capture that human experience.</u>
