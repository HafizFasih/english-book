## SECTION A — READING COMPREHENSION *(CLO 2)*

### Passage: *Why We Must Teach Children to Understand Feelings?*

Emotional literacy, the ability to recognise, understand, express and regulate emotions in oneself and others, is increasingly recognised as a fundamental component of human development. While cognitive and academic skills have traditionally dominated educational priorities, recent research emphasises that emotional understanding in early childhood significantly influences long-term social, psychological and academic outcomes.

From birth, infants communicate basic emotional states through non-verbal cues such as crying, smiling and body movements. However, as children enter preschool and primary education, they encounter complex social situations that require more than instinctive reactions. Conflicts with peers, disappointment with academic tasks, and interactions with authority figures demand the capacity to identify specific emotions and respond appropriately. Children who lack these skills often resort to aggression, withdrawal or prolonged distress.

Developing emotional literacy involves several key processes. First, children must acquire an adequate emotional vocabulary. When a young child experiences frustration, an adult's intervention such as "You seem angry because the tower fell" not only labels the emotion but also links it to its cause. Longitudinal studies demonstrate that children exposed to explicit emotion labelling by age four display greater emotional regulation by age seven.

Second, narrative practices play a critical role. Reading stories or watching scenarios that depict characters experiencing joy, fear, sadness or anger enables children to adopt the perspective of others—a skill known as theory of mind. Guided questions such as "How do you think the character feels?" and "What could help them feel better?" encourage empathy and moral reasoning.

Third, structured social play provides practical training in negotiation and compromise. When two children desire the same resource, adult facilitation that highlights mutual feelings ("How does it feel when someone takes your toy?") transforms potential conflict into a learning opportunity about fairness and reciprocity.

Empirical evidence supports the value of systematic emotional education. A controlled study involving kindergarten children who participated in a ten-week emotional literacy programme showed marked reductions in disruptive behaviour and significant improvements in peer relationships compared to a control group. Teachers reported enhanced classroom climate and increased student engagement.

Parental modelling is equally influential. Adults who articulate their own emotions ("I feel disappointed because the meeting was cancelled, so I need a few minutes alone") demonstrate that emotional experience is universal and manageable. Conversely, suppressing or aggressively expressing emotions teaches children that feelings are either shameful or uncontrollable.

Several education systems have integrated emotional literacy into formal curricula. In Finland, daily "emotion check-ins" allow students to name their current emotional state in a non-judgmental environment. Longitudinal data from such programmes correlate with higher levels of student well-being and lower incidence of bullying.

Despite resource constraints, even brief daily interventions yield results. Simple classroom tools—emotion charts, role-play activities, or short reflective discussions—require minimal preparation yet foster substantial growth in emotional competence.

Ultimately, emotional literacy is not an optional "soft skill". It underpins conflict resolution, mental health, and social cohesion. Educational systems that prioritise emotional education in early years invest in more cooperative, empathetic and psychologically healthy future generations.

Write an essay of **200–250 words** on ONE topic:
- a. "How Social Media Affects Gen Z Friendships"
- b. "Why Mental Health Days Should Be Normal for Gen Z"

<details>
<summary>View Answer — Topic (b): Why Mental Health Days Should Be Normal for Gen Z</summary>

**Why Mental Health Days Should Be Normal for Gen Z**

We take a day off when we have a fever. We rest when our back hurts. Yet, when our mind is exhausted, overwhelmed, or breaking down, we are expected to carry on as if nothing is wrong. <u>Mental health days should be completely normal for Gen Z, because mental well-being is just as important as physical health.</u>

<u>First of all</u>, Gen Z faces levels of stress that no previous generation has experienced. Between academic pressure, social media comparison, financial uncertainty, and a global pandemic, young people today are mentally stretched to their limits. Research shows that anxiety and depression rates among teenagers have risen sharply over the past decade. Taking a planned mental health day allows students to recharge, reflect, and return to their responsibilities with renewed focus.

<u>Furthermore</u>, normalizing mental health days reduces the stigma around mental illness. When schools and workplaces treat a "mental health day" the same way as a "sick day," it sends a powerful message: your mind matters. Students feel seen, supported, and less alone in their struggles.

In conclusion, Gen Z deserves the right to take care of their mental health without shame or judgment. Schools and employers should formally recognize mental health days as a basic need, not a luxury. A healthy mind is the foundation of everything else.
