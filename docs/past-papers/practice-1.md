## Question 1 — Reading Comprehension *(14 marks)* [CLO-2]

Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions that follow.

### Passage: *The Science of Procrastination*

Everyone knows the feeling. You have an important assignment due tomorrow, yet you find yourself reorganising your desk, checking social media, or making your third cup of tea. This is procrastination — the **tendency** to delay tasks that need to be done. While it may seem harmless, research has revealed that procrastination is far more **complex** than simple laziness. It is, in fact, a deeply psychological issue rooted in how humans manage emotions.

Scientists at Carleton University in Canada conducted a study in which students who **identified** themselves as procrastinators were tracked over an entire semester. The findings were striking. Not only did these students perform worse academically, but they also reported higher levels of stress, illness, and regret toward the end of the semester. The researchers concluded that procrastination was not a time management problem, but an emotion regulation problem. When people face a task that causes anxiety, boredom, or self-doubt, they instinctively avoid it to feel better in the short term.

The brain plays a significant role in this cycle. The limbic system — the part of the brain responsible for emotions and immediate pleasure — constantly battles the prefrontal cortex, which handles planning and long-term thinking. When the limbic system wins, a person chooses short-term comfort over long-term **achievement**. This is why scrolling through social media feels easier than writing an essay, even when the person knows the essay is far more important.

Interestingly, not all procrastination is the same. Researchers distinguish between passive procrastinators, who delay tasks due to inability to decide or act, and active procrastinators, who choose to delay because they work better under pressure. Studies show that active procrastinators often produce quality results at the last minute and experience less stress than passive ones. However, this habit remains risky, as unexpected events can completely **disrupt** even the best-laid last-minute plans.

So how can procrastination be overcome? Experts recommend breaking large tasks into smaller, manageable steps. Starting with just two minutes of focused work on a task — known as the "two-minute rule" — is often enough to overcome the initial **resistance** and build momentum. Other strategies include setting specific deadlines, removing digital distractions, and rewarding yourself after completing each small step. The key insight is that procrastination is not a character flaw — it is a habit, and like all habits, it can be changed with conscious effort and the right strategies.
