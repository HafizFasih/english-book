## SECTION A — READING COMPREHENSION *(CLO 2)*

### Passage: *Social Media and the Mental Health of Young People*

In the last decade, social media has become a central part of daily life for billions of people, especially teenagers and young adults. Platforms like Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, and Twitter allow users to share moments, connect with friends, and access information instantly. While these platforms offer genuine benefits, a growing body of research is raising serious concerns about their **impact** on the mental health of young users.

One of the most well-documented effects is the rise of **social comparison**. When people scroll through social media feeds, they are constantly exposed to carefully edited and filtered images of other people's lives — their achievements, vacations, relationships, and appearances. Research from the University of Pennsylvania found that reducing social media use to just 30 minutes per day led to significant reductions in feelings of loneliness and depression among university students. The problem is not the platform itself, but the habit of **passive** consumption — endlessly scrolling and comparing without actively engaging.

Sleep **deprivation** is another serious consequence of heavy social media use. Studies show that teenagers who use their phones for more than three hours a day are significantly more likely to suffer from poor sleep quality. The blue light emitted by screens interferes with the production of melatonin, the hormone responsible for regulating sleep. Poor sleep, in turn, is directly linked to increased anxiety, poor concentration, and weakened emotional resilience.

However, social media is not entirely negative. When used **intentionally**, it can provide valuable social support, especially for young people who feel isolated or misunderstood in their physical environment. Online communities for mental health, for example, have helped many young people feel less alone and more willing to seek professional help. The key distinction appears to be between active use — creating content, chatting with friends, joining communities — and passive use — silently watching other people's lives without interacting.

Experts recommend that young people set clear daily limits on social media use, avoid screens for at least one hour before sleeping, regularly take "digital detox" days, and curate their feed to include only content that is uplifting and educational. Parents and educators also have a responsibility to teach children digital literacy — the ability to navigate online spaces critically and safely.
