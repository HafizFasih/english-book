## Q2 — Reading Comprehension *(14 marks)*

### Passage: *The Growth Mindset*

Think back to the time when you were in a classroom, maybe a Math classroom, and the teacher gave the class a difficult problem. Which of the two following responses is closer to the way you reacted?

- **A.** Oh no, this is too hard for me. I'm not even going to seriously try and work it out.
- **B.** Oh, this is quite tricky but I like to push myself. Even if I don't get the answer right, maybe I'll learn something in the attempt.

Early in her career, the psychologist Carol Dweck of Stanford University gave a group of ten-year-olds problems that were slightly too hard for them. One group reacted positively, said they loved challenge and understood that their abilities could be developed. She says they had a growth mindset and are focused on what they can achieve in the future. But another group of children felt that their intelligence was being judged and they had failed. They had a fixed mindset and were unable to imagine improving. Some of these children said they might cheat in the future; others looked for someone who had done worse than them to boost their self-esteem.

Professor Dweck believes that there is a problem in education at the moment. For years, children have been praised for their intelligence or talent, but this makes them vulnerable to failure. They become performance-oriented, wanting to please by getting high grades, but they are not necessarily interested in learning for its own sake. The solution, according to Dweck, is to praise the process that children are engaged in—making an effort, using learning strategies, persevering and improving. That way they will become mastery-oriented (interested in getting better at something) and will achieve more. She contends that sustained effort is the key to outstanding achievement.

Psychologists have been testing these theories. Students were taught that if they left their comfort zone and learned something new and difficult, the neurons in their brains would form stronger connections, making them more intelligent. These students made faster progress than a control group. In another study in an underperforming school, children on a Native American reservation were exposed to growth mindset techniques for a year. The results were nothing less than staggering. They came top in regional tests, beating children from much more privileged backgrounds. These children had previously felt that making an effort was a sign of stupidity, but they came to see it as the key to learning.

So, back to our original question. If you answered B, well done—you already have a growth mindset. If A, don't worry—everyone is capable of becoming mastery-oriented with a little effort and self-awareness.

### (a) Answer the following questions *(4 marks)*

- **(i)** What facts are included in the second paragraph?
- **(ii)** What can you infer about "growth mindset" after reading the second paragraph?
- **(iii)** What facts are included in the third paragraph?
- **(iv)** What inferences can you make about "growth mindset"?

<details>
<summary>View Answers</summary>

**(i)** The second paragraph presents facts from Carol Dweck's research at Stanford University. She gave a group of ten-year-olds problems that were slightly too hard for them. One group loved the challenge and believed their abilities could grow (growth mindset). The other group felt judged and believed their intelligence was fixed, leading some to consider cheating.

**(ii)** From the second paragraph, we can infer that a growth mindset leads to a positive attitude toward challenges. Children with a growth mindset do not fear failure — they see it as an opportunity to learn and improve. They focus on future achievement rather than current performance.

**(iii)** The third paragraph presents the fact that praising children for their intelligence rather than their effort is harmful. It also presents Dweck's solution: praise the process (effort, strategies, perseverance) rather than natural talent. This makes children mastery-oriented and more likely to succeed.

**(iv)** We can infer that a growth mindset can be taught. The way adults respond to children's efforts directly shapes their mindset. Children praised for hard work rather than intelligence become more resilient, motivated, and ultimately more successful learners.

## Q3 — Précis Writing *(5 marks)*

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

**Title: The Power of a Growth Mindset**

Psychologist Carol Dweck of Stanford University had discovered two types of mindsets in children: a fixed mindset, where children believed their intelligence could not change, and a growth mindset, where children believed they could improve through effort. Research showed that praising children for their intelligence made them fearful of failure, while praising them for their effort made them more motivated and resilient. Experiments proved that teaching students about brain development led to faster progress. Even children from disadvantaged backgrounds showed remarkable improvement when exposed to growth mindset techniques. The author concluded that everyone was capable of developing a growth mindset through effort and self-awareness.

<details>
<summary>View Answer — Sport is the Most Powerful International Language</summary>

**Sport is the Most Powerful International Language**

When two countries are in political conflict, their athletes may still shake hands on the same field. When people cannot communicate in words, they can still share the excitement of a goal or a finishing line. <u>Sport is the most powerful international language because it breaks cultural barriers, promotes peace, and connects people of all nationalities without the need for words.</u>

<u>Sport breaks down cultural and political barriers</u> in ways that diplomacy often cannot. The Olympic Games bring together athletes from nearly 200 countries, many of whom are in conflict with each other. <u>For example</u>, "ping-pong diplomacy" between the United States and China in the 1970s helped reduce tensions between the two superpowers, showing how sport can open doors that politics cannot.

<u>Furthermore</u>, sport promotes values that are universally understood — fairness, teamwork, hard work, and respect. A young boy in Brazil and a young girl in Japan may not speak the same language, but both can understand the thrill of scoring a goal. <u>In addition</u>, events like the FIFA World Cup and the Cricket World Cup bring billions of people together in shared celebration.

<u>Some critics argue that sport can also divide rather than unite people</u>, pointing to examples like nationalist violence at football matches or political boycotts of the Olympics. This is a fair concern. <u>However</u>, these incidents are the exception, not the rule. The overwhelming majority of sporting events — from local school competitions to world championships — bring people together in a spirit of fair competition and mutual respect. The rare cases of division do not outweigh the millions of moments of unity that sport creates every single day.

In conclusion, no other human activity reaches across languages, cultures, and political boundaries the way sport does. <u>Sport may not solve every conflict, but it remains the world's most inclusive and powerful language — one that every human being instinctively understands.</u>

*(Word count: 290)*

</details>
