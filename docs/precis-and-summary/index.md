# Chapter 10 — Précis & Summary Writing

Both a **précis** and a **summary** condense a longer text into a shorter form, capturing the essential ideas without distortion. While closely related, they have distinct rules and purposes.

---

## 10.1 What Is a Précis?

The word **précis** (pronounced *"Preisi"*) comes from French, meaning *"cut short."* A précis is:

- A **concise summary** or **miniature version** of a passage
- Includes all essential points, the author's mood and tone, and the main idea or gist
- A **condensed version** — typically **one-third** the length of the original
- Written in the précis writer's **own words**
- Always in **third person** and **past tense**
- **Objective** — contains no personal opinions or reactions

---

## 10.2 Approach to Writing a Précis

Ask yourself:
1. What is the basic theme of the passage?
2. Which are the most important points?
3. What points can I eliminate?

### Steps

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **Step 1** | Read the passage carefully. Underline or highlight key points in the same order as the original. |
| **Step 2** | Identify the central theme, main idea, and the author's tone. |
| **Step 3** | Re-read and compare with your notes. Check nothing crucial was missed. |
| **Step 4** | Give the précis an appropriate title. |
| **Step 5** | Draft the précis from your notes, keeping it to one-third of the original length. |

---

## 10.3 Rules for Writing a Précis

:::note Rules to Follow
- Write in the précis writer's **own words** — do not copy sentences from the original
- Include all **essential points** and the author's tone, facts, and opinions
- Follow the **logical order** of the original passage
- Write in **third person** and **past tense**
- Do not include **abbreviations or contractions**
- Do not add **opinions, reactions, or interpretations** not found in the original
- **Officials** should be referred to by designation, not name
- Avoid phrases like *"In my opinion"* or *"I think"*
:::

### A Good Précis Is:

- **Clear** — easy to understand
- **Concise** — no unnecessary words
- **Coherent** — ideas flow logically
- **Precise** — accurate to the original
- **Well-structured** — no grammar or language errors

## 10.4 What Is a Summary?

A **summary** condenses the main ideas of a text so its readers will understand the gist of the original work. It is:

- Written in the **author's own words**
- Typically **one-quarter to one-third** the length of the original
- Written in **third person**
- May sometimes quote a particularly effective word or phrase (in quotation marks)

### Getting Started with a Summary

1. **Write down the text's main point** in one sentence — identify the text, the writer, what the writer does (reports, explores, analyses, argues), and the most important point.
2. **Divide the text into sections** using signal phrases and topic shifts as guides.
3. **In one or two sentences, sum up each section** — these become your topic sentences.
4. **Combine** your main-point sentence with your section summaries. This is your first draft.
5. Read the draft. Add, remove, or revise as needed.

---

## 10.5 Objective vs. Critical Summary

| Type | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Objective Summary** | Condenses main ideas only, with no opinion or evaluation. Readers draw their own conclusions. |
| **Critical Summary** | Condenses information *and* comments on the effectiveness and quality of the original work. Requires the writer to evaluate and support their assessment. |

---

## 10.6 Guidelines for Summary Writing

- The summary **identifies the original work** by title and author in the first sentence
- It credits the original source with publication information (author, title, genre, publisher, date)
- It does **not** introduce new information
- It does **not** repeat examples verbatim — paraphrase instead
- Follow the specific instructions for your assignment

---

## 10.7 Sample Summary

**Original work:** "No Allusions in the Classroom" by Jaime O'Neill (*Newsweek*, 23 Sept. 1985)

---

> Author Jaime O'Neill's article, "No Allusions in the Classroom," **emphasises the communication problem between teachers and students due to the students' lack of basic knowledge.** The author supports this assertion by using a combination of personal experience, evidence from recent polls, other professors' opinions, and the results of an experiment conducted in his own classroom. The experiment O'Neill conducted was an ungraded eighty-six-question "general knowledge" test issued to students on the first day of classes. On this test, "most students answered incorrectly far more often than they answered correctly." Incorrect answers included fallacies such as: "Darwin invented gravity" and "Leningrad was in Jamaica." Compounding the problem, students do not ask questions, meaning their teachers assume knowledge the students do not have. O'Neill shows the scope of this problem by demonstrating that, according to their teachers, this is a typical problem across the United States. O'Neill argues that common knowledge is essential for communication and that without it, learning becomes far more difficult because teacher and student do not share a common body of knowledge.

---

*Works Cited:*
O'Neill, Jaime. "No Allusions in the Classroom." *Newsweek*, 23 Sept. 1985.

---

## 10.8 Précis vs. Summary — Quick Comparison

| Feature | Précis | Summary |
|---------|--------|---------|
| Length | One-third of original | One-quarter to one-third |
| Tense | Always past tense | Can use present or past |
| Title | Always given | Optional |
| Source credit | Not required | Required (in first sentence) |
| Tone preservation | Required | Not required |
| Quotations | Not used | Occasionally permitted |

---

---

## 10.9 Step 6 — Review Your Précis

After drafting, review your précis to confirm that you have:
- Included all essential points, supporting evidence, and the gist of the original passage
- Maintained coherence and correctness with respect to grammar, spelling, and clarity
- Stayed within one-third of the total word count of the original

## 10.10 Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Nigeria and Rice Imports

**Original Passage:**

Nigeria spends an average of US $22 billion each year on food imports. Its major food imports include wheat, sugar, and fish. Another big import, rice, accounts for about US$1.65 billion. Most of the country's rice is imported from Thailand and India. This has led analysts to predict it will be the world's second-largest importer of rice after China in 2019. There are a few reasons that Nigeria's demand for rice is so high. Among them is rapid urbanisation; people who arrive in cities and seek out cheap, nutritious, filling food invariably turn to rice. More traditional coarse grains like sorghum and millet have become less popular over the years. This, according to research, is because rice is "a more convenient and easy staple to prepare compared to other traditional cereals across income levels in the urban areas." Now Nigeria's government is trying to stem the tide of imported rice. Towards the end of 2018, it announced its intention to halt rice imports entirely. In doing so, it hopes to save a great deal of money. But is this plan realistic? Unfortunately, it's not. Nigeria's agricultural sector simply isn't equipped to produce the amount of rice to meet demand. It is battling natural and climatic factors, a lack of funding and training for farmers, and a dearth of government support. To turn this around, the government needs to invest heavily in rice farmers.

**Précis — Nigeria and its Import of Rice**

There are reasons for the high demand for rice in Nigeria, which makes it import $1.65 billion worth of rice out of a total of $22 billion in food imports each year, and it is predicted to become the world's second-largest importer of rice after China in 2019. Rapid urbanisation, cheapness, and nutritional value are some of the reasons for its high demand. The choice of rice is because of its convenience and easiness of preparation. The Government is putting a blanket ban on rice imports, as announced towards the end of 2018, in order to save money. However, the plan is unrealistic by design since Nigeria's agricultural sector is not prepared to meet its rice demand. On top of that, Nigeria is battling other factors too, including natural and climatic factors, dearth of funding, and farmers' training. To solve these problems, the Government should invest in rice farmers aggressively.

### Example 2 — Social Animals

**Original Passage:**

When we survey our lives and efforts we soon observe that almost the whole of our actions and desires are bound up with the existence of other human beings. We notice that the whole nature resembles that of the social animals. We eat food that others have produced, wear clothes that others have made, and live in houses that others have built. The greater part of our knowledge and beliefs has been passed on to us by other people through the medium of a language that others have created. Without language and mental capacities, we would have been poor indeed, comparable to higher animals.

We have, therefore, to admit that we owe our principal knowledge over the least to the fact of living in human society. The individual if left alone from birth would remain primitive and beast-like in his thoughts and feelings to a degree that we can hardly imagine. The individual is what he is and has the significance that he has, not much in virtue of his individuality, but rather as a member of a great human community, which directs his material and spiritual existence from the cradle to the grave.

**Précis:**

Being social animals, human beings have their actions and desires bound up with society. In matters of food, clothes, knowledge, and belief they are interdependent. They use language created by others. Without language, their mental power would not grow. They are superior to beasts because they live in human society. An individual life left alone from birth would grow utterly beast-like. So human society, and not individuality, guides man's material and spiritual existence.

### Example 3 — Teaching is the Noblest Profession

**Original Passage:**

Teaching is the noblest of professions. A teacher has a sacred duty to perform. It is he on whom rests the responsibility of moulding the character of young children. Apart from developing their intellect, he can inculcate in them qualities of good citizenship, remaining neat and clean, talking decently, and sitting properly. These virtues are not easy to be imbibed. Only he who himself leads a life of simplicity, purity, and rigid discipline can successfully cultivate these habits in his pupils.

Besides, a teacher always remains young. He may grow old in age, but not in spirit. Perpetual contact with budding youths keeps him happy and cheerful. There are moments when domestic worries weigh heavily on his mind, but the delightful company of innocent children makes him overcome his transient moods of despair.

**Précis:**

Teaching is the noblest profession. A teacher, himself leading a simple, pure, and disciplined life, can mould the character of young children and make them neat and well-mannered citizens. Besides, he remains very young, forgetting his own domestic worries in the constant company of the young.

### Example 4 — Digital Literacy (Précis and Critical Summary)

**Original Passage — "Digital Literacy: Skills, Challenges, and Contexts"**

a. Digital literacy refers to the ability to effectively and responsibly use digital technologies to access, evaluate, create, and communicate information. In today's interconnected world, it is not limited to basic computer skills but encompasses a broad range of competencies, including online research, critical thinking, and digital communication. Being digitally literate means understanding how technology influences society and how individuals can navigate digital spaces safely and productively.

b. One of the key aspects of digital literacy is information evaluation. With the vast amount of data available online, individuals must be able to distinguish between credible sources and misinformation. This requires critical thinking skills, awareness of bias, and the ability to cross-check facts. Without these skills, people risk being misled by false information, which can have serious consequences in areas such as health, politics, and education.

c. Digital literacy also involves responsible participation in online communities. This includes understanding digital etiquette, respecting privacy, and practising safe online behaviour. Cybersecurity awareness — such as recognising phishing attempts or safeguarding personal data — is an essential component. Moreover, digital literacy empowers individuals to use technology for collaboration, creativity, and innovation.

d. Finally, digital literacy is a lifelong skill that evolves with technological advancements. As new tools and platforms emerge, individuals must continuously adapt and learn. Educational institutions and workplaces play a vital role in fostering digital literacy, ensuring that people are prepared to thrive in a digital economy. Ultimately, digital literacy is not just about using technology — it is about using it wisely, ethically, and effectively.

**Précis (Objective, 1/3 Length):**

In the text *"Digital Literacy: Skills, Challenges, and Contexts"*, the author states that digital literacy is the responsible use of technology. He points out that it goes beyond basic computer skills, incorporating critical thinking, information evaluation, and safe online participation. Moreover, the author explains that it enables individuals to identify credible sources, avoid misinformation, and engage ethically in digital communities. Finally, the author concludes that digital literacy is a lifelong skill essential for adapting to technological change and succeeding in modern society.

**Critical Summary (Analytical, with Quotation):**

The writer states that digital literacy is a vital skill in the modern age, as reflected in the text *"Digital Literacy: Skills, Challenges, and Contexts."* He states that it involves evaluating information, practising safe online behaviour, and engaging ethically in digital communities. However, a critical perspective reveals that this view emphasises individual responsibility while overlooking structural barriers such as the digital divide, where marginalised groups lack access to reliable internet or devices. Furthermore, the author's portrayal of digital literacy as universally attainable does not fully address ethical dilemmas posed by surveillance technologies and algorithmic bias. As the author notes, *"digital literacy is not just about using technology — it is about using it wisely, ethically, and effectively."* Therefore, while digital literacy is indeed crucial, it must also be understood within broader social, economic, and political contexts to ensure equity and fairness.
